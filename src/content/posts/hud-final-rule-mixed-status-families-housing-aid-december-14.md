The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development published a final rule in the [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/11/12/2026-23003/housing-and-community-development-act-of-1980-verification-of-eligible-status) on Thursday, November 12, that requires every member of a household in covered HUD rental-assistance programs to verify U.S. citizenship or eligible immigration status "regardless of age." The rule (FR Doc. 2026-23003, 91 FR 72978) takes effect December 14, 2026, and is signed by HUD Secretary Scott Turner.

The rule's second change targets "mixed-status" families, households where some members are eligible for aid and some are not. Under HUD's previous regulations, those families could receive prorated assistance, covering only eligible members, indefinitely. The final rule makes prorated assistance "a temporary condition pending verification of eligible status of all family members, where permitted by statute," per the [rule's summary](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/11/12/2026-23003/housing-and-community-development-act-of-1980-verification-of-eligible-status). HUD [announced the rule](https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-091) on November 10, two days before publication.

## What changes for tenants

Under the old rules, family members who declared themselves U.S. citizens did not have to provide extra documentation, the rule text notes. The final rule changes that. HUD says primary verification of citizenship will run through the federal SAVE system, so many households will not need to submit documents, and it estimates about 99 percent of existing U.S.-citizen tenants have already been verified, according to the [rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-11-12/pdf/2026-23003.pdf).

HUD says the December 14 effective date "does not require all existing households to complete the new verification process on that date," and that "the majority of households will have a year or more to submit evidence of U.S. citizenship." The final rule lets housing providers grant an extension of up to 60 days for people having trouble getting documents. Families with ineligible members can still ask for a temporary deferral of termination: an initial period of up to six months, renewable in six-month steps up to 18 months total, "affording additional time to arrange alternative housing," the rule says. No termination can happen before an informal hearing process is completed.

## HUD's own numbers

HUD's Regulatory Impact Analysis, as summarized in the [final rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/11/12/2026-23003/housing-and-community-development-act-of-1980-verification-of-eligible-status), estimates that about 20,000 mixed-status families received assistance as of December 2024. Children under 18 account for roughly 65 percent of the eligible family members in those households, and families with children make up about 16,000, or 78 percent, of the affected families, HUD says.

HUD calls the rule's main effect a transfer: about $167 million to $218 million a year in rental assistance moving from mixed-status families to fully eligible households on waiting lists. It puts the genuine costs at $17 million to $33 million, largely one-time. HUD also writes that it "does not take lightly the impact this rule will have on children in mixed-status households," while arguing that Section 214 of the Housing and Community Development Act of 1980 does not allow continued assistance for anyone whose eligible status hasn't been verified.

HUD first proposed the rule on February 20, 2026. The comment period closed April 21, and HUD says it received 16,238 comments.

## What supporters and critics say

"President Trump warned that the days of illegal aliens and ineligible noncitizens exploiting government-funded welfare programs would be over," Turner said in [HUD's release](https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-091). "It's unacceptable that America's homeless veterans, disabled, elderly, and victims of domestic violence languish on waitlists while illegals and ineligibles scam our system." HUD's release says a HUD and DHS audit found nearly 200,000 tenants with incomplete or unknown eligibility verification, and that HUD-funded housing serves only a quarter of eligible households in need.

Critics say the rule will break up families. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities estimates about 80,000 people in roughly 20,000 mixed-status families could be affected, including about 37,000 children who are mostly U.S. citizens, [CBS News reported](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/hud-public-housing-citizenship-immigration-rule/). "This is tearing apart families and sowing division," Marie Claire Tran-Leung, managing attorney at the National Housing Law Project, told CBS. A coalition of 22 state attorneys general who opposed the rule estimated more than 7,000 affected mixed-status households in California and 2,500 in New York, according to CBS.

Mixed-status families have been allowed to live together in HUD-funded housing since 1995, as long as subsidies covered only eligible members, CBS reported. HUD tried a similar change in 2019; that proposal was withdrawn in 2021. Tran-Leung told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/12/nx-s1-6037699/new-rule-could-push-out-of-subsidized-housing-tens-of-thousands-of-u-s-children) a legal challenge is likely.
