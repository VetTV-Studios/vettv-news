The U.S. Army on Wednesday morning, November 25, carried out a controlled detonation of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa. The blast went off at about 9 a.m. CT, [Iowa Public Radio reported](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2026-11-24/reynolds-iowa-lead-azide-detonation-army-ammunitions-plant-ernst-grassley-middletown), the time the Army had [announced](https://www.army.mil/article/297069/army_announces_controlled_detonation_of_degraded_explosive_material_at_iowa_army_ammunition_plant) a day earlier.

The Army says the material, a highly sensitive explosive used to set off other explosives, had degraded so badly that moving it, opening it or treating it with chemicals was too dangerous. Federal officials argued that a planned, mitigated blast was safer than waiting for the material to go off on its own as temperatures dropped.

## What was destroyed and why

According to the [Army](https://www.army.mil/article/297069/army_announces_controlled_detonation_of_degraded_explosive_material_at_iowa_army_ammunition_plant), inspectors found severe degradation and crystallization in about 68 drums of lead azide stored inside earth-covered magazines, totaling more than 9,700 pounds of net explosive weight, or just under 5 tons. In that state, the Army said, lead azide becomes "an ultra-sensitive primary explosive vulnerable to minimal friction, vibration, heat, or shock."

The 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals laid out more of the history in its [November 23 opinion](https://ecf.ca8.uscourts.gov/opndir/26/11/263374P.pdf):

- **Storage:** The drums had been kept in two earth-covered magazines "for over two decades" in a stabilizing solution of water and ethanol.
- **March 2026:** The Army determined the lead azide had recrystallized and become unstable.
- **November 13:** The EPA issued an emergency permit that, for the first time, authorized blowing up the material in place. The permit runs through February 11, 2027.
- **November 15:** The Army announced plans for detonations on November 20 at noon.

"There is consensus across the experts, across the country, that a controlled, mitigated detonation of the lead azide here at Iowa Army Ammunition Plant is far preferable to an uncontrolled, spontaneous detonation of that substance," Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle said at a Tuesday news conference, [according to the Army](https://www.army.mil/article/297069/army_announces_controlled_detonation_of_degraded_explosive_material_at_iowa_army_ammunition_plant).

Col. Aaron Teller, commander of the 71st Explosive Ordnance Disposal Group, said crystallized lead azide had built up on the outside of the barrels and their lids. "It prevents those barrels from being able to be moved," he said, which is why the material could not be neutralized chemically, the [Army](https://www.army.mil/article/297069/army_announces_controlled_detonation_of_degraded_explosive_material_at_iowa_army_ammunition_plant) reported.

ATF Director Robert Cekada said the Iowa plant is, as far as the bureau knows, the only place in the U.S. still holding a lead azide stockpile, [Iowa Public Radio reported](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2026-11-24/reynolds-iowa-lead-azide-detonation-army-ammunitions-plant-ernst-grassley-middletown). Telle said he has ordered a review of every Army weapons storage facility "to ensure that something like this never happens again," according to [IPR](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2026-11-24/reynolds-iowa-lead-azide-detonation-army-ammunitions-plant-ernst-grassley-middletown).

## The court fight

The Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club, represented by Earthjustice, sued on November 19 to challenge the EPA permit. They argued it would let the Army create "a plume of lead dust that will blanket homes and communities, possibly as far away as 100 miles or more" with less than a week's notice, according to the [8th Circuit opinion](https://ecf.ca8.uscourts.gov/opndir/26/11/263374P.pdf). The Army had already moved the blast from November 20 to November 23 that same day. The court put the permit on hold on November 20, and on November 22 the Army [reset the date](https://www.army.mil/article/296990/army_update_on_iowa_army_ammunition_plant) to November 25, citing weather and mitigation work.

On Monday, a panel of Judges Bobby Shepherd, L. Steven Grasz and Justin D. Smith denied the groups' emergency motion and lifted the hold in an unsigned [per curiam opinion](https://ecf.ca8.uscourts.gov/opndir/26/11/263374P.pdf). The judges found the groups had not shown they were likely to win, saying the permit "adequately incorporated safeguards to minimize the dispersal of lead dust." They added: "We are mindful of the very real risks presented by the Army's planned detonation," but the degraded explosives "present a risk of an uncontrolled and unplanned explosion, which would occur without the benefit of the safety measures that the Permit puts in place."

The groups said they would keep pursuing their challenge to the EPA permit. "Detonation, once a last resort, is now the only option remaining," the [Iowa Environmental Council said](https://www.iaenvironment.org/newsroom/council-news/eighth-circuit-decision), adding that "the Army's negligence has directly created this public health emergency." The groups' underlying petition for review is still pending.

## Trump, the governor-elect and Iowa's officials

Before the court ruled Monday, President Donald Trump [posted on Truth Social](https://truthsocial.com/@realDonaldTrump/posts/117667060434959655) that a controlled detonation was "the proper course," but that he had "ordered the Military not to do so until such time as the Eighth Circuit agrees that we can go forward." He also made the decision "fully subject to the incoming Governor Elect, Rob Sand, agreeing to the operation," and said that if Sand or the courts did not agree, he would hold the incoming governor "responsible for any and all death or destruction."

Sand, a Democrat who takes office in January, answered that the administration "should make this decision based on the information they have from the people they brought in to do the job months ago," and asked federal officials to share their briefing information with the public, in a post on X [quoted by KWQC](https://www.kwqc.com/2026/11/23/trump-backs-controlled-iowa-blast-says-court-gov-elect-sand-must-agree/). Telle later called the go-ahead a consensus decision, [NPR and Iowa Public Radio reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/24/nx-s1-6051547/court-lifts-administrative-stay-allowing-lead-azide-detonation-to-move-forward).

Republican Gov. Kim Reynolds backed the plan on Tuesday. "I take the Army's warning seriously that the choice is between a planned detonation or an uncontrolled explosion," she said in a [statement](https://governor.iowa.gov/press-release/2026-11-24/gov-reynolds-issues-statement-supporting-planned-detonation). "I believe, based on the information I've been given, that the danger is immediate."

Iowa's two Republican U.S. senators both supported a controlled detonation while criticizing how the Army communicated. Sen. Joni Ernst said in a statement that "the military owes the people it serves more transparency, not less,", and Sen. Chuck Grassley said the Army's October 15 public meeting in Middletown fell short, [Iowa Public Radio reported](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2026-11-24/reynolds-iowa-lead-azide-detonation-army-ammunitions-plant-ernst-grassley-middletown).

## Safety steps and what happens next

The [Army](https://www.army.mil/article/297069/army_announces_controlled_detonation_of_degraded_explosive_material_at_iowa_army_ammunition_plant) said the magazines were barricaded with sand-filled barriers about two stories high, along with water tanks, foam, gel blocks and misting systems meant to hold lead dust close to the site. FEMA supported a recommended relocation for residents of Middletown and a five-mile zone southwest of the plant, with hotel stays funded through Friday, November 27. People farther out in the advisory area were told to stay inside with windows and doors closed.

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/epa-collects-over-1200-air-land-and-water-samples-ahead-iowa-army-ammunition-plant) said Wednesday morning, before the blast, that it had collected 1,216 water, soil, dust and air samples, had about 300 staff and contractors in the area, and that pre-blast sampling detected "no lead currently in the air." The agency said it would post air monitoring data every five minutes during the event and keep sampling afterward.

One hour after the detonation, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/initial-air-monitoring-shows-zero-detection-airborne-lead-following-controlled) said "all U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) real-time air monitors are reporting zero detection of airborne lead throughout the region." The agency said its monitors "never stopped collecting data throughout the event." EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin said that "over the course of the upcoming minutes, hours, and days," about 300 EPA employees and contractors "will be conducting extensive and thorough monitoring, taking samples of air, land, and water, and deploying remediation efforts if necessary."

Gov. Kim Reynolds, in a [Wednesday statement](https://governor.iowa.gov/press-release/2026-11-25/gov-reynolds-issues-statement-following-successful-detonation-iowa-army-ammunition-plant), said "the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency has confirmed zero detection of airborne lead in the region" and called that "a best-case scenario for the residents of Middletown and its surrounding communities." She also said Iowa agencies "will continue to monitor the situation in the days ahead as more information related to air, soil, and water quality becomes available."

[Iowa Public Radio](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2026-11-24/reynolds-iowa-lead-azide-detonation-army-ammunitions-plant-ernst-grassley-middletown), in an update at 12:55 p.m. CT, reported that Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley of the U.S. Army Sustainment Command said in a livestreamed address that overhead coverage of the explosion showed all lead azide barrels were "definitively removed," and that there were no secondary explosions, kickout or infrastructure damage. Shirley said a "visible signature" of the detonation was largely caused by more than 3,600 gallons of aqueous foam applied to mitigate the blast. FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton said, according to IPR, that preliminary evidence from the EPA and other sources showed a "0.0 detection level of lead in the atmosphere." IPR also reported that President Donald Trump posted on Truth Social, "Early reports from Iowa are that the Army's mitigation efforts have far exceeded expectations."

IPR reported there had been no official all-clear for evacuated residents. Hamilton, IPR said, asked people to stay tuned with local public health officials, and said evacuees will be contacted by phone once the all-clear is given.

According to the [Army](https://www.army.mil/article/297069/army_announces_controlled_detonation_of_degraded_explosive_material_at_iowa_army_ammunition_plant), any residential property that tests above the RCRA-approved standard of 200 parts per million of lead will have soil removed, surfaces cleaned and the property restored "at complete government expense." The U.S. Army Claims Service will handle claims for damage from blast vibrations. After the all-clear, the Army advises residents to wet-wipe outdoor surfaces such as decks, windowsills and play equipment.

Affected residents can register for help at DisasterAssistance.gov or call the FEMA Helpline at 1-800-621-3362, the [Army](https://www.army.mil/article/297069/army_announces_controlled_detonation_of_degraded_explosive_material_at_iowa_army_ammunition_plant) said.

This is a developing story. The EPA's initial real-time air-monitor readings are not a completed soil or water assessment, and neither the EPA nor the Army has issued an all-clear.
