Epic Games says Apple has produced **less than 1%** of the documents required in their fight over App Store fees for outbound, or "link-out," purchases. Apple says that figure is the result of **overbroad search terms**, not a decision to withhold responsive files.

The claims are in two court filings in *Epic Games, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, No. 4:20-cv-05640, in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California: [Epic's November 13 motion to enforce a production order](https://9to5mac.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/2026/11/gov.uscourts.cand_.364265.1727.0.pdf) and [Apple's November 18 opposition](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.364265/gov.uscourts.cand.364265.1731.0.pdf). [AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/26/11/24/apple-hands-epic-less-than-1-of-required-documents-in-its-app-store-fee-fight) and [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/11/17/epic-accuses-apple-of-withholding-122000-documents-seeks-daily-fines/) reported on the dispute; [9to5Mac also covered Apple's response](https://9to5mac.com/2026/11/19/apple-fires-back-at-epic-over-claims-it-withheld-122000-documents/) on November 19.

As of this writing on the morning of November 24, we have **not** seen a final order deciding the motion. Epic noticed the motion for a November 24 hearing. We are describing the filings, not a ruling.

## What Epic's motion says

A prior court order, quoted in Epic's motion, required Apple to "produce to Epic all non-privileged documents relating to the decision-making process leading to the proposal in its proffer, including any fee proposal reflected in its proffer, as well as a privilege log." Epic says that production was due by **August 24, 2026**. According to the motion, Apple "produced only 34 unique documents by that deadline, and 680 as of today."

The dispute then narrowed to search terms run against custodians' files. Epic says Apple is "withholding well over 99% of the documents returned by running the agreed-to search terms" on the files of the four people Apple identified as deciding the proposed fee — **Tim Cook, Eddy Cue, Carson Oliver, and Kevan Parekh**, Apple's CFO — plus three others: **Nate Barton, Alex Roman, and Phil Schiller**.

Epic's comparison, from the motion: "while Apple produced **632** custodial documents returned by these search terms (including families), Apple is withholding **over 122,000** based on a claim of non-responsiveness."

Epic also says the production from those seven "Initial Custodians," which Apple has called substantially complete, still does not include "a single substantive email discussing the rates Apple ultimately proposed," notes or communications from meetings where the rates were discussed or decided, an internal analysis of the financial or business impact, App Store profit-and-loss statements, or support for Apple's assertion that its incremental link-out costs are "essentially zero."

Apple, Epic says, agreed to search the files of **all 27** of Epic's proposed custodians, to substantially complete production for the seven initial custodians by November 1, and to substantially complete the remaining **20** custodians by **November 25, 2026**.

Epic asks the court to order Apple to produce, by **December 2, 2026**, "all non-privileged documents that hit on any of the Parties' agreed-upon search terms ... irrespective of any relevance or responsiveness assessment," and to impose "a daily monetary sanction for each day Apple fails to comply after December 2, 2026." The motion does not name a dollar amount for that sanction.

## What Apple's opposition says

Apple's November 18 opposition does not dispute the scale of the hit list. In a footnote, Apple defines the "Initial Custodian Document Population" as "**the 122,884 unique documents** from the seven current or former Apple employees that Epic identified as priority custodians" that "contain hits on one or more of Epic's search strings." Apple says it has "substantially completed productions for these custodians" and that productions for the remaining 20 "remain ongoing."

Apple also restates Epic's critique: that the "responsiveness rate across the Initial Custodian Document Population was 'less than 1%.'" Apple's answer is that "that rate ... is demonstrably a function of Epic's overbroad search terms and custodians, not evidence that Apple withheld responsive documents."

The opposition gives examples. It says "fully half" of Epic's search strings contain terms such as `fee*`, and that `fee*` "pulled in documents containing words like 'feel,' 'feed,' 'feedback,' 'feet,' and 'feeble' without the word 'fee' or 'fees' appearing at all." It says a `commission*` term "captures documents that refer to government and private commissions, as well as documents hitting on the totally irrelevant term 'commissioner,' such as documents discussing the NFL Commissioner."

Apple writes that "at least 95% of the Initial Custodian Document Population hit on generic terms like these and did not include any reference to the term 'link-out' or its variations." Those terms, Apple says, also "captured highly sensitive and irrelevant documents, such as Apple employees' personal tax or financial records, medical records, documents discussing unannounced products, documents containing passwords, investor-relations materials, and privileged or protected legal communications." Later in the brief, Apple also lists "performance reviews and compensation information."

Apple's legal argument is that "parties are only required to produce responsive documents, not every document returned by a search string." It says Epic's proposed remedy — producing every non-privileged hit by December 2, without a responsiveness review — would be unduly burdensome and "impossible to comply with by December 2."

Apple asks the court to **deny** Epic's motion. In the alternative, if the court wants some relief, Apple "submits that it should order the production, by December 2, of a randomized, statistically significant sample of nonresponsive documents to a Special Master for review consistent with Epic's document requests subject to Apple's objections."

## How to read the two sides

These are advocacy filings. Epic is asking a court to treat a very low production rate as a failure to comply with an existing order. Apple is asking the court to treat that same rate as the predictable result of broad search strings, and to keep the ordinary responsiveness filter in place, with a Special Master sample as a fallback.

[AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/26/11/24/apple-hands-epic-less-than-1-of-required-documents-in-its-app-store-fee-fight), writing on November 24, also described the 122,884-document figure, the December 2 request, and Apple's overbreadth and Special Master arguments. Where that article discusses what "the court" thinks of the rate, we are not repeating those characterizations here, because the two primary filings we reviewed are the parties' briefs, not a decision.

*Our take (opinion):* the number both sides are arguing over is now on the record, in Apple's own opposition: 122,884 unique search-term hits from seven custodians, with Epic saying 632 of those were produced. What a court will require Apple to turn over, and by when, is still the question in front of the court.
